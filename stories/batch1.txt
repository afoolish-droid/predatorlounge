The Back Booth

Renata came in the first time because the hospital cafeteria closed at midnight and the Sunset Dreams Lounge did not. She ordered tea. The bartender laughed, kindly, and made her one anyway in a rocks glass with a lemon wheel, and that was how Vincent noticed her.

He did not come over that night. He came over the fourth night, after she had stopped bringing a book. He set down a plate of olives she had not ordered and said, "Night shift," not a question. She asked how he knew. He touched his own wrist where her watch was, the cheap rubber kind nurses wear because they get washed so often. "My mother," he said. "Thirty years at Mercy."

She did not check. Why would she check.

The back booth was the only one with a curtain. Regulars talked about it the way people talk about a table at a famous restaurant, half joking, half not. On the second Thursday in November Vincent held the curtain open for her and said she looked like someone who needed to sit somewhere nobody could ask her for anything. Inside it was warm and smelled of cedar. There was a lamp with a fringed shade. He did not sit down with her. That was the clever part, she would think much later. He let her have it alone.

After that the booth was hers on Thursdays. The bartender, Ines, would catch her eye when she came in and tilt her head toward the curtain, and Renata felt a little foolish and a little chosen, which is a combination that is hard to give up.

He started joining her in December. Ten minutes, then twenty. He asked about patients and remembered their names. He remembered Mr. Abernathy, who wanted his dog brought in, and asked a week later if the dog had come. No one at home asked her about Mr. Abernathy. Her husband had stopped asking about the hospital around the time she stopped asking about his job, an even trade.

"You give everything away," Vincent said one night. "Who gives anything to you?"

She said nobody, as a joke. He did not laugh. He just nodded, slowly, like a doctor writing something down.

By January she was telling him things she had not told her husband. That she was thinking of leaving hospice. That she sometimes sat in the parking garage for forty minutes before going inside her own house. He listened with his whole face. Once, when she cried, he did not hand her a napkin. He waited until she reached for one herself, and then said, "There she is."

In February she came in on a Tuesday, because things at home were bad, and the curtain on the back booth was closed. She could hear a woman's voice behind it, soft, laughing at something. Ines would not look at her.

Vincent came out ten minutes later and found her at the bar. He put his hand on her shoulder, warm, and said, quietly enough that only she could hear, "Thursdays, sweetheart. You know that."

She did know that. She found she was grateful he had reminded her.

On the House

You made partner on a Wednesday and nobody had planned anything, which was fine, which you told yourself was fine, so you walked eleven blocks in the rain to the only neon you could see. The Sunset Dreams Lounge. The sign buzzed like it was clearing its throat.

You ordered the most expensive scotch on the shelf because you could. The man at the end of the bar, silver at the temples, sleeves rolled, told the bartender to put it on the house. "Congratulations," he said. You had not told anyone. "You've got the look," he said. "First time you've won something you can't explain to your parents."

That was Ray. He owned the place. He said it like an apology.

You came back Friday. The scotch was on the house again. Ray had saved you the stool by the window, a little card on it that said RESERVED in gold script. He asked about the firm, the cases, the senior partner who called you kiddo. He laughed in the right places. He said, "They're scared of you. That's what kiddo means."

You had never thought of it that way. You thought of it that way for weeks.

It was always on the house. You tried to pay and he put his hand flat over your card on the bar and slid it back to you, and the gesture had a weight to it, a pleasant weight, like a coat someone sets on your shoulders. Your friends from law school came once. Ray was cool to them. Not rude. Just cool. Afterward he said, "They talk about you like you're still twenty-four," and you realized they did.

They did not come again. You did not invite them.

In March you brought a client. Ray comped the whole table and sent over oysters, and the client said you must be someone here, and you felt it in your chest. Afterward, at the bar, Ray slid a small leather folder toward you. Inside was a handwritten ledger. Every drink since November. Every oyster. The total was underlined twice.

"Don't look like that," he said, smiling. "I'm not asking for it. I just keep things. I keep everybody's things." He closed the folder and tucked it into his jacket. "It's good to know where we stand with each other."

You said you would pay it. He looked hurt. He said that would insult him. He said you were the only person in this room he trusted, and that he wanted to ask you something, just a question about a lease, nothing, something a lawyer could look at over a drink.

You looked at it over a drink. Then over several. The questions got larger. You began billing your hours at the firm a little thinner to make time.

In June the senior partner called you kiddo and you did not feel anything at all. You were thinking about Ray, whether he had saved the stool, whether he was annoyed with you for coming in late on Tuesday.

He was. He did not say so. He just let you pay, for the first time, and watched you sign.

Residency

My agent said two weeks at the Sunset Dreams Lounge was beneath me, and she was right, and I took it anyway because the rent on my apartment does not care what is beneath me.

Dorian met me at the stage door with a space heater. That was the first thing. A little ceramic heater, plugged in already, aimed at the stool where I'd sit between sets. "Singers get cold," he said. "Nobody thinks about it." In nineteen years of clubs nobody had.

The room was small and amber and smelled like orange peel. The piano was tuned. The light he gave me was perfect, a single warm spot that made my skin look like I'd slept. I sang better than I had in a year. He stood at the back, by the coat check, every set, and never once looked at his phone.

After the first week he asked if I'd stay a third. Then a fourth. He said the room had changed since I came. He said, "They don't come for the drinks anymore. They come for you." I knew better than to believe that and I believed it.

There were things. Small things. My keyboard player, Hollis, wanted a Saturday off for his daughter's recital, and Dorian said of course, and then on Saturday the sound was off and the light was wrong, too blue, and three tables walked out, and Dorian found me afterward and said, gently, "It's not the same without the band you trust, is it." I let Hollis go the next week. Dorian found me a new player. A kid who did exactly what he was told.

He started coming to my dressing room before shows. He'd sit in the doorway, one foot in the hall, always one foot in the hall. He'd tell me which song to open with. He was always right. He'd tell me which dress. He was usually right. He told me my agent didn't understand my voice, that she was selling me like a product, and I fired her in January and let him handle the bookings, which mostly meant there weren't any outside the lounge.

I don't remember deciding to stop going home between sets. My apartment was forty minutes away and there was a cot in the storeroom and he'd put a quilt on it, a nice one, hand stitched. "My grandmother's," he said.

My contract was up in March. I asked him about renewal and he looked at me with real sorrow and said the owners were cutting costs. I said, you're the owner. He said, "Not of everything."

I sang that night like I was begging. The spotlight felt hot. He stood by the coat check and did not look at his phone, and at the end of the last set he gave a single slow clap, alone, until the room joined in.

He renewed me the next morning, at half the rate, and I thanked him. I actually thanked him. I hugged him in the doorway and he patted my back twice, the way you pat a dog that has finally learned to sit.

What I Saw from the Well

From behind the bar at the Sunset Dreams Lounge you can see every booth reflected in the mirror, if you know how to tilt your head. I have worked the well for six years. I know how to tilt my head.

The woman with the laptop started coming in September. Thirty, maybe, a doctoral student named Meredith, writing about medieval Portuguese trade routes, which she explained to me once, generously, while I cut limes. She took the corner booth by the outlet and drank seltzer with bitters and tipped like someone who had once worked a bar.

Lyle noticed her in the third week. I saw him notice. He has a way of going still.

He did not approach her. He told me to start bringing her the seltzer before she asked. He told me to turn the music down a notch when she was typing. When the outlet in her booth stopped working, it was because he had me flip the breaker, and when she asked about it, he came over himself with an extension cord and a little apology and stayed four minutes. I timed it. He always stays four minutes the first time.

Then he started reading. I would find books on the end of the bar, history books, Portuguese ones. He left them where she would see. He asked her one night, as if he'd just thought of it, whether she knew anything about the spice trade.

I watched her face open like a window.

By November they were talking an hour at a time. He is a good listener; that is not the lie. He let her explain things to him he already knew. He found her an archive, a private collector, letters. He knew a man. He always knows a man. The man would only meet at the lounge, after close.

I was not on those nights. Lyle changed the schedule.

In December her advisor emailed her something unkind, and she cried in the booth, and Lyle sat across from her and said the advisor was threatened, said the department did not deserve her, said maybe she didn't need them. I saw her nod. I saw her close the laptop, and she did not open it again in the lounge after that. She came to talk. Only to talk.

In February she asked me, while I was cutting limes, whether I thought Lyle was a good man. She asked it lightly. She had stopped tipping like someone who'd worked a bar. She had stopped paying at all.

I tilted my head toward the mirror and saw him at the end of the bar, watching us. Not angry. Just still.

I said yes. I said he was very generous.

She smiled like I'd given her something. Then she went back to her booth, and Lyle came down the bar and put a twenty in my tip jar, folded once, the way he always does when I've done well.

We, the Regulars

We noticed Albert before Marcus did, or we like to think so. He came in the winter after his wife died, a tall man in a camel coat who sat close to the piano and didn't drink what he ordered. We knew the type. Some of us had been the type.

He came for the piano. Sundays, the house player did standards, and Albert would mouth the words to "Skylark" with his eyes closed. We left him alone. That is the courtesy of the Sunset Dreams Lounge. Nobody asks.

Marcus asked.

We watched him do it, one Sunday in February, carrying a drink Albert hadn't ordered and setting it down with two fingers like a chess move. He said Ruth used to request that song. We looked at one another. None of us had told him about Ruth. Albert had never said her name aloud in that room.

Later we figured out it was the obituary. Marcus reads them. He keeps a stack behind the register, clipped, and some of us knew that and some of us didn't, and all of us would have said it was sweet, before.

Albert began coming Wednesdays too. Then Fridays. Marcus moved the piano a foot closer to his table and told the house player to learn the rest of Ruth's songs, which Marcus somehow knew, all of them. Albert cried during "Moonglow" and Marcus sat with him, and we turned our faces politely toward the bar.

Albert's son came in once, in April. A heavy, worried man. He asked Albert to come to dinner. Marcus brought the son a drink on the house and talked to him about grief, about how everyone grieves differently, about how some fathers need a place that isn't home. The son left alone. Albert watched him go and said nothing, and Marcus put a hand on his shoulder and said, "He means well."

We all heard the shape of that sentence. We didn't say so.

In June Albert gave Marcus the piano. Ruth's piano, a Steinway from their living room, delivered on a Tuesday by three men in gray uniforms. Marcus said he couldn't possibly accept it, and accepted it, and had a little brass plate made: IN MEMORY OF RUTH. The house player plays it now. It sounds better than the old one.

Albert stopped coming in September. We assumed he had died, or moved in with his son. Marcus said he'd gone to a home, said it sadly, said he visited. Some of us believed him.

What we know is that the brass plate is gone now. We asked once. Marcus said it was tarnishing. He said he'd have a new one made.

On Sundays a woman in a black dress sits close to the piano and mouths the words with her eyes closed. Recently widowed, we hear. Marcus brought her a drink she didn't order. We turned our faces politely toward the bar.
