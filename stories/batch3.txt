Top Shelf

Frank Delaney said he only came in for the bourbon, and for two years that was true. The Sunset Dreams Lounge kept a good shelf, and Rocco, who owned it, poured heavy and didn't talk, which was all Frank wanted from a bar on the way home from the plant.

The new bartender, Jules, talked.

Not a lot. She asked how his day was and then she actually waited for an answer. That was new. Frank found himself telling her about his foreman. Then his daughter's tuition. Then, one night in March, after the third pour, about the woman from accounting he'd had lunch with twice, just lunch, nothing, but he'd liked it more than he should, and he hadn't mentioned it to Carol.

Jules nodded and wiped the bar. She didn't make a face. He went home lighter.

The next night Rocco poured him a bourbon he hadn't ordered, a better one, from the top shelf. He leaned on the bar for the first time in two years. "Lunch," Rocco said. "There's no crime in lunch." Then he winked and walked away.

Frank sat very still.

He didn't come in for a week. Then he did, because Carol was angry about the tuition, because the plant was laying people off, because where else. Rocco greeted him by name. He poured the top shelf again. He asked about the foreman, by name. He asked about the daughter's school, by name. Every single thing Frank had told Jules, Rocco knew, and he said each one warmly, carefully, the way you'd hand back a man's wallet after he dropped it. Look what I'm holding for you.

Frank stopped talking to Jules. Rocco talked to Frank instead. Rocco was good at it. He said, "Your wife doesn't understand what that plant does to a man." He said, "You deserve one place where nobody needs anything from you." Frank found he agreed. He began coming every night. He began calling Carol from the parking lot to say he was working late.

In June Rocco asked him for a favor. His nephew needed a job. Something at the plant. Frank was a supervisor now, after the layoffs; he'd survived them, and he'd half wondered why. Rocco had known a man at corporate. Rocco always knew a man.

Frank hired the nephew. Then the nephew's friend. Then, in August, Rocco asked whether the loading dock was locked on Sundays, just curious, just a question, and Frank said he didn't know, and Rocco poured the top shelf and said, "Find out for me."

Frank looked at him for a long moment. Rocco looked back pleasantly.

"How's Carol?" Rocco asked. "Still doesn't know about lunch?"

There had only been the two lunches. There had never been anything. But Frank could hear how it would sound in Rocco's voice, that warm, careful voice, and he understood suddenly that it would not matter what was true.

He found out about the dock. He came back the next night and told Rocco, quietly, and Rocco squeezed his hand on the bar and said, "There he is." Jules was cutting limes. She did not look up.

Now That Is a Mind

Thursday trivia at the Sunset Dreams Lounge had been running for twenty-two years, and Margaret Coyle's team, the Dewey Decimators, had won it nine times. Four retired teachers and a librarian. They sat at the round table under the mirror and brought their own pencils.

Desmond took over the lounge in the spring, and the first thing he did was host trivia himself.

He was wonderful at it. Funny, quick, a little wicked. He wrote harder questions. He learned everyone's names in a week. He singled Margaret out the first night, after she got a question about Byzantine coinage nobody else in the room could touch. "Now that," he said into the microphone, "is a mind." The room applauded. She blushed like a girl.

He started asking her to check his questions in advance. Just her. He'd bring the cards to her table on Tuesdays and they'd go over them with coffee, and he deferred to her on everything, and she found herself looking forward to Tuesdays in a way she hadn't looked forward to anything since she retired.

One Tuesday he mentioned that Phyllis had been asking whether Margaret got the questions early. He said it with a little laugh. He said he'd told Phyllis of course not. "But you know Phyllis," he said. Margaret did know Phyllis. Thirty years at the same school. She hadn't known Phyllis was the type.

At trivia that week Phyllis was cool to her. Margaret was cool back. The Decimators came in third.

The next Tuesday Desmond said Ruth had told him Margaret was getting a little forgetful. He said he'd defended her. He said, "I told her, that woman knew Byzantine coinage cold." He said it to make her feel better and it did, and underneath it something else, a small cold stone, settled in her stomach and stayed.

She stopped meeting the Decimators for lunch. They stopped calling. By summer she was playing trivia at the bar, alone, on a team Desmond had named for her: Margaret Alone. He said it was a joke, affectionately. He announced her score first, before the teams. She won three weeks running. People clapped politely and didn't look at her.

The Decimators broke up in August. Phyllis's husband was sick; Ruth was moving. Margaret heard it from Desmond. "They didn't tell you?" he asked, very gently. "I'm so sorry. I'd have thought."

In September he asked if she'd consider helping with the books. Just the bookkeeping. She'd been so good with the questions. A woman with her mind, wasted at home. She said yes before he finished.

Thursday nights she sits at the round table under the mirror by herself now. Desmond calls her the house champion. She has her own pencil. Sometimes, in the mirror, she sees a new face at another table answer something difficult, and she watches Desmond lean into the microphone and say, "Now that is a mind," and she grips her pencil until her knuckles go white, and she doesn't know who she's angry at.

The Members' Room

Councilwoman Teresa Vance came to the Sunset Dreams Lounge for the quiet. She told Gil that on her first night, and he nodded gravely and made sure no one ever approached her table. Not constituents, not reporters, not the occasional drunk who recognized her from the news. Gil handled it. A word, a hand at the elbow, a drink on the house, and they drifted away.

She had not had quiet in four years.

In the fall he showed her the room upstairs. A members' room, he called it, though she never saw a list of members. Low leather chairs, a fire, a window over the river. He gave her a brass key on a red tassel. "For when the quiet downstairs isn't quiet enough," he said.

She went up alone at first. She read briefs by the fire. Gil would knock once, bring a pot of tea, and leave. Then he'd knock once and stay. He knew about zoning, it turned out. He knew about the riverfront development. He had opinions, careful ones, which he offered as questions.

She found herself saying things in that room she didn't say in chambers.

Other people had keys. She learned that in December, when she came up and found a developer named Pruitt by the fire, a man who'd been in her office twice that month. Gil was pouring him a drink. Gil looked up and smiled with real warmth and said, "Teresa, you know Pruitt." She said she did. She should have turned around. Gil had already poured her a glass.

It was a pleasant hour. Nothing was discussed. That was the strange part. Pruitt talked about his grandchildren, about sailing. Gil laughed at the right moments. When she stood to go, Gil helped her with her coat and said softly, "See? People are only people up here."

After that there were others. A union man. A judge. A woman from the state's attorney's office. Gil introduced her to each one like a gift. Never any business. Just the fire, the river, a drink, and the brass keys on their red tassels set on the side table like a little collection.

In March a reporter called her office. Was it true she'd met privately with Pruitt during the riverfront vote. She said no. The reporter said there was a photograph. She said that was impossible. The reporter said the photograph showed her by a fire, laughing, Pruitt's hand on her arm.

She went to the lounge that night and up the stairs, and Gil was waiting by the fire with tea already poured. He looked stricken. He said he'd find out who did it. He said he'd protect her. He said, "You know I'd never let anything happen to you up here." He took her hand.

She believed him. That was what she could never explain to anyone afterward. She sat by the fire and let him hold her hand, and she believed him, and when he asked, very gently, how she planned to vote on the river parcel next month, she told him.

Rides Home

October 3. Theo auditions for the house piano gig at the Sunset Dreams Lounge and plays too fast, the way he always does when he's nervous. Elias, the owner, listens from a barstool with his eyes closed. Afterward he says, "You play like a man running from something." Then he says, "Can I drive you home? It's raining." Theo's bike has a flat. Theo says yes.

October 3, later. Elias's car is old and immaculate and smells of leather and cloves. He doesn't play the radio. He asks who taught Theo, and Theo tells him about Mrs. Lindqvist, about the conservatory he dropped out of, about his father, who called the piano a girl's thing until the day he died. Elias drives the long way. Theo notices and doesn't mind.

October 10. Theo gets the gig. Elias drives him home again, though it isn't raining. "Your bike's not safe on Ridge Avenue," he says. Theo doesn't argue.

October 24. Elias stops outside Theo's building and doesn't unlock the doors right away. He says he's been thinking about Theo's father. He says some men don't know how to love what they don't understand. Theo sits in the dark car and something in his throat closes and opens. Elias unlocks the doors. He doesn't say good night. He says, "Same time Friday."

November 7. Theo mentions he's been asked to sub at a wedding, a good fee. Elias is quiet the whole drive. At the curb he says he's glad for him, really. He says the lounge can survive one Saturday without him. At the wedding Theo plays well and thinks about the silence in that car the entire time.

November 8. Elias doesn't pick him up. Theo rides his bike on Ridge Avenue in the rain and gets clipped by a cab mirror, nothing serious, a bruise. When he gets to the lounge Elias sees the scrape on his arm and goes pale and takes him into the office and cleans it himself with a cloth and says, "This is my fault. I should never have let you go." Theo apologizes. He isn't sure for what.

November 21. Theo's bike is gone from the alley. Stolen, Elias says. Terrible neighborhood. He'll drive Theo every night now. It's settled.

December 5. Theo asks to be dropped at a friend's place instead of home. Elias says sure. Elias waits outside the friend's building with the engine running for two hours. When Theo comes out, Elias has a coffee for him, still hot, and doesn't mention the time.

December 19. Theo stops seeing the friend. It's easier.

January 9. Theo plays "Für Elise" between sets as a joke, the way Mrs. Lindqvist used to make him. Elias laughs harder than anyone. On the drive home he says, "I'd never have called it a girl's thing." Theo feels warm for a mile.

January 9, later. Elias drives past Theo's building without slowing. Theo says, that's my stop. Elias says he knows. He says he just wants to drive a little longer. He says there's no one waiting up for either of them, is there, and Theo realizes it's true, and leans his head against the window, and lets the car carry him wherever it's going.

Twenty-Seven

June and Walter Pike have come to the Sunset Dreams Lounge on every anniversary for twenty-six years. Same booth, same Manhattans, same song on the jukebox, which Walter plays with a quarter he keeps in his wallet all year for exactly that purpose.

Harlan bought the lounge last spring. On their anniversary he sent over a bottle of champagne and a note in fountain pen: TO TWENTY-SIX MORE. June kept the note.

They started coming in more often. Harlan made it easy. He remembered them. He sat with them. He told stories about the old owner that made Walter laugh until he coughed. June hadn't heard Walter laugh like that since his retirement.

The first strange thing happened in September. Walter had three Manhattans instead of two and said something sharp about June's sister, and June said something sharp back, and they went home quiet. The next week Harlan sat down in their booth, and when Walter went to the restroom, Harlan put his hand over June's and said, "Are you all right? After last week?"

June said it was nothing. A spat. Harlan frowned in a kind way. "He grabbed your wrist, June," he said softly. "I saw it from the bar."

She was certain he hadn't. Almost certain. She looked at her wrist. Harlan squeezed her hand and let go before Walter came back.

In October Harlan stopped Walter at the coat check. June saw them talking, and on the drive home Walter said, out of nowhere, "Harlan says you were flirting with the bartender." June laughed. Walter didn't. "He says you do it every time I go to the restroom." June said that was insane. Walter said, "Is it?" and looked at the road.

After that Harlan told each of them things. Quietly, separately, with great sorrow. What the other had said after a third drink. What the other had done when they thought nobody was looking. He was never cruel about it. He seemed to hurt for them. He always ended the same way: "I just thought you should know. I care about you two."

June started writing things down in a little notebook in her purse, what she'd said, what she'd done, to have proof. She showed it to Harlan once, hoping he'd see. He read it carefully, then closed it and handed it back and said, "June. Sweetheart. People who write things down are usually afraid of what they did."

Walter moved into the guest room in December. Neither of them could have said exactly why.

They still come to the lounge. Separately, now. June on Tuesdays, Walter on Fridays. Harlan sits with each of them and listens and tells them they're not crazy, it's the other one, and they both feel understood for the first time in months.

On their twenty-seventh anniversary June came in alone and sat in the booth. Harlan sent over champagne. The note in fountain pen said TO YOU.

Somebody had put a quarter in the jukebox. Their song was playing. Walter was at the end of the bar, not looking at her. Harlan stood between them, smiling, a hand on each of their shoulders, as if posing for a photograph.
