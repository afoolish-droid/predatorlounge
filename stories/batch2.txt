Contact Sheet

The Sunset Dreams Lounge had a photographer on Fridays, an old-fashioned touch Hana liked. A young man named Pip with a film camera who took pictures of couples and sold the prints for ten dollars at the end of the night. Teddy, the owner, called it the house tradition. He said people had forgotten how to be looked at.

Hana had been divorced for five months. She had been looked at, lately, mostly by lawyers.

Pip took her picture the first Friday she came in alone. She laughed and covered her face and he took it anyway. At the end of the night Teddy brought her the print in a little paper sleeve. In the photo she was laughing into her hand and she looked about thirty-two. "On the house," he said. "Nobody should have to buy proof they're beautiful."

She put it on her refrigerator. She hadn't had anything on her refrigerator since Daniel took the magnets, which, he'd argued, were his mother's.

She came most Fridays after that. Pip photographed her at the bar, in the booth, dancing once with a man from out of town, and Teddy brought her the prints, always on the house, always with a sentence. "Look at your shoulders here." "That's a woman who's done apologizing." She started dressing for the camera. She did not quite admit it to herself.

In the spring Teddy asked if she'd like to see the archive. Upstairs, a narrow room with flat files and a light table. Forty years of Friday nights. He pulled out drawers and showed her strangers laughing in 1987, kissing in 1994. Then he opened a drawer with her name on the tab.

There were many more than she'd been given. Contact sheets. Frames she'd never seen: her checking her phone, her eyes swollen in the bathroom mirror through the half-open door, her asleep for a moment in the back booth, mouth open. Her talking to the man from out of town, closer than she remembered standing. Her in the parking lot, which she had not known was a place Pip went.

"I keep everything," Teddy said quietly. "It's how I take care of people. Nobody ever loses a night."

She felt the room tilt. She said it was a lot. He agreed that it was. He said Daniel's lawyers would probably love an archive like this, wouldn't they, a woman alone in a bar every Friday, and then he laughed and closed the drawer and said, "Not that anyone will ever see it. They're mine. They're safe with me."

She thanked him. She didn't know what else to say.

She still comes on Fridays. She sits where the light is good. Teddy brings her a print at the end of the night, on the house, always with a sentence, and she always says thank you, and she smiles at Pip when he raises the camera, because it would be strange not to, and because Teddy is watching to see if she does.

In Reverse

Sunday. Joaquin stands behind the bar of the Sunset Dreams Lounge in a black vest that fits him perfectly, because Sal had it tailored, and he pours a bourbon for a man he used to cook for. The man does not recognize him. Sal, at the end of the bar, raises his glass an inch. Joaquin smiles back. He does not remember deciding to smile.

Two weeks before. The landlord's letter on the counter of the restaurant next door, which is no longer Joaquin's restaurant, which is no longer anyone's. Sal reads it over his shoulder and says, "You don't owe me a thing. You know that." Joaquin knows that. Sal has told him a hundred times. Sal slides something across the bar. Not an apron. A vest.

A month before. The health inspector, unannounced, on a Thursday. Somebody had called. Joaquin never found out who. Sal brought him a coffee afterward and sat with him on the curb and said, "People are jealous of a man with a gift." He said it like he was angry on Joaquin's behalf. He paid the fine himself, wouldn't hear of anything else, and Joaquin cried on the curb, and Sal held the back of his neck.

Three months before. The second loan, to replace the walk-in. Sal wrote the check at the bar with a fountain pen and wouldn't take a note. "A handshake," he said. "We're neighbors." He asked, while he was blotting the ink, whether Joaquin's sister had found work yet, and when Joaquin said no, Sal made a call, and she had a job by Monday at a hotel Sal knew. Joaquin's mother sent Sal a tin of polvorones.

Five months before. Sal coming next door for the first time and ordering everything on the menu. Sending notes back to the kitchen on cocktail napkins, saying which dishes were genius and which were merely very good. He was always right. Joaquin kept the napkins in a drawer.

Six months before. The first loan. Two thousand dollars, the week the produce supplier cut him off. "Pay me back when it's easy," Sal said. "And if it's never easy, it's never easy." Joaquin laughed. It was a relief to laugh.

Eight months before. Joaquin, closing late, smoking in the alley between the restaurant and the lounge. Sal came out the lounge's back door with two glasses and handed him one. "I've been watching you," he said. "The way you come out here after service like a man coming up for air." He said, "You're too good for this block." Joaquin felt seen, which is a feeling like warmth, like being handed a drink in the cold.

A year before. Sal at the front window of the lounge, watching a sign go up next door. A new restaurant. A young chef carrying crates in from a van, alone, sleeves rolled, whistling. Sal watched the whole afternoon. He sent the bartender over with a bottle of champagne and a card that said WELCOME, NEIGHBOR. He did not sign it. He wanted to see what the young man would do with something good he couldn't explain.

Last Call

Every night at the Sunset Dreams Lounge ends the same way. At one-forty Conrad dims the house lights to a quarter. At one-forty-five he plays the same record, a Julie London side, and the regulars know it means finish up. At two he locks the front door and pours one drink, always for the same person, and the staff count out.

When Noor started in August, the drink was for Bettina, who had waited tables there eleven years.

Noor was saving for culinary school. She had a jar at home with a label that said PARIS, written in her own hand as a joke she half meant. She told Conrad on her first night, when he asked what she wanted out of life, and he didn't laugh. He asked which school. He asked what she'd cook first. He remembered the answer the next week. Tarte Tatin. Her grandmother's pan.

Her schedule was good at first. Then it changed. Thursday doubles. Sunday closes. A shift on her day off, as a favor. Conrad apologized every time. He said she was the only one he trusted on a Saturday. He said Bettina was slowing down. He said it like a secret between them, and she kept it.

In October, at two in the morning, Conrad poured the drink for Noor.

Bettina was still counting her tips at the end of the bar. She did not look up. She folded her bills, stood, and put her coat on slowly, and on the way out she touched Noor's shoulder, very lightly, the way you touch someone at a funeral.

Noor drank it. Rye, something sweet. Conrad watched her. He said she had good instincts. He said real chefs learn in rooms like this, not in Paris, not from people who'd never had to close. He said, "You know what they'd teach you there? To be like everyone else."

She stayed later. The drink became two. He talked about the restaurant he'd open someday, upstairs, twelve seats, and he needed someone with instincts, and he looked at her when he said it.

She stopped putting money in the jar in November. There didn't seem to be a reason. She spent it on knives instead, good ones, a set he recommended, from a man he knew.

Bettina didn't come back after New Year's. Conrad said she'd moved to Phoenix, near her daughter. Noor texted her, and the message sat there, delivered, unanswered.

In February a new hire started. Lucía. She was studying for the nursing boards. She had a picture of her son taped inside her locker. Conrad asked her on her first night what she wanted out of life, and Noor, rolling silverware at the end of the bar, heard the softness come into his voice, the same exact softness, and felt something in her stomach that was not quite jealousy and not quite fear.

At one-forty the lights went down. At one-forty-five, Julie London. At two, the lock.

Conrad poured one drink. He held it a moment, just long enough for both of them to see him hesitate.

Then he slid it to Noor. She took it. She was so grateful she could have wept.

The Blackout

9:12 p.m. The grid goes down across the east side of the city, and the Sunset Dreams Lounge goes dark with it, mid-song. Someone screams, then laughs. Graham, who is in town for six weeks auditing a regional bank and has been drinking alone here for four of them, feels a hand on his forearm in the dark. "Stay put," says Augustin. "It's safer inside."

9:30. Candles. Augustin has dozens, as if he's been waiting. He sets them on every table and the room turns gold and old-fashioned and everyone becomes better looking. Phones are at ten percent. Augustin says the bar is open, cash or tab, honor system, and people cheer.

10:15. The street outside is black. A siren, far away. Augustin locks the front door and pockets the key. "Looters," he says, apologetically. Nobody objects. There is a feeling in the room like a snow day.

11:00. Augustin sits with Graham. He knows Graham's name; he has always known it. He asks about the bank. Graham shouldn't say anything and says a little. Augustin listens like it's the most interesting thing he's ever heard. He says, "You're the only honest man in that building, aren't you." Graham hadn't thought of it that way.

11:40. A couple asks to leave. Augustin unlocks the door for them, and the dark outside is so complete that they come back in after a minute, laughing nervously. Augustin locks it again. "See," he says to Graham, as if it proves something.

12:30 a.m. Graham has a dead phone and a warm drink. Augustin has told him about his late wife, about his bad heart, about how he has no one to leave the lounge to. He asks if Graham has ever thought about staying in a town like this. A smaller life. A man could buy into a place like this, he says. A man with a head for numbers.

1:15. A woman at the bar, a regular, says something sharp to Augustin about the door. He smiles and refills her glass and says nothing, and ten minutes later she is crying in the restroom and nobody knows why. When she comes out she apologizes to him. He accepts.

2:00. Augustin shows Graham the books. By candlelight, in the office, the ledger open on the desk. Something about it is wrong in a way Graham could find in an hour. Augustin says, "I've never shown anyone this." He says, "I trust you." He pours two more.

3:30. Graham wakes on the leather couch in the office with his jacket over him like a blanket, the ledger closed, and Augustin sitting across from him, watching, perfectly composed. "You talked a lot," Augustin says kindly. "About the bank. About things you probably shouldn't have." He shrugs. "Don't worry. I won't remember any of it if you don't."

5:50. The power returns all at once, every light, the jukebox mid-song. Graham blinks at the brightness. Augustin unlocks the front door, holds it open for him, squeezes his shoulder, and says, "Same time tomorrow?"

Graham says yes before he can think. The street is gray and ordinary. He has five weeks left in town.

Forty

I knew she'd been stood up before she did. You learn. She came in at seven-thirty in a green dress that was too much for a Tuesday, told my bartender she was meeting someone, ordered two glasses of prosecco, and set the second one across from her like a placeholder. By eight-fifteen she'd turned her phone face down. By eight-forty she'd drunk the second glass.

I sent over a slice of cake with a single candle. I didn't know it was her birthday. You'd be amazed how often it is.

She laughed and covered her mouth and looked around for who had done it, and I let her find me, and I raised my glass. Forty, she said when I came over. Forty and stood up at a bar called Sunset Dreams. You couldn't write it. I said I'd read worse. She laughed again. People like to laugh at the thing that's hurting them; you just have to give them permission.

Her name was Celeste. She taught middle school orchestra. The man had been from an app. I told her I'd had my heart broken in this very room in 1991, which isn't true, but it was the right story, and she leaned in.

I'm not a monster. I want to be clear about that. I make people feel good. That's my whole business. The only question is what it costs and who keeps the receipt.

She came back Thursday. I'd saved her table. I'd had my bartender learn her drink. When she sat down I said, "Celeste," like it was a word I'd been practicing, and I watched her shoulders drop an inch. That inch is everything. I've built a life on that inch.

I let her talk about the app men, the violin students, her sister who has three kids and a husband who brings her coffee in bed. I agreed her sister had it easy. I agreed the men were idiots. I agreed, gently, that most people didn't see her. Then I said the thing I always say, which is, "I see you," and you'd think a grown woman would hear how cheap that is. They never do. Nobody's ever said it to them in a room this warm.

By spring she was here four nights a week. She'd stopped dating. Why bother, she said. She'd stopped calling her sister so much. Her sister was judgmental, I agreed. I gave her a key to the back door so she could come in early and play the piano when the room was empty. She wept when I handed it to her.

I keep the key ring in my desk. There are eleven others on it.

Her birthday's coming again. Forty-one. I'll have a cake. One candle. I'll tell her she doesn't look a day older and I'll mean it, because she doesn't; she looks exactly the way I want her to look, which is like someone who has nowhere else to go.

You're wondering why I'm telling you this. You came in alone, didn't you. You've been looking at your phone.

Let me get you something. On the house.
