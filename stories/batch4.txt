The Dome

Isaac wrote his poems at the end of the bar at the Sunset Dreams Lounge because it was the only place in the city he'd never been interrupted. Cyril, the owner, made sure of it. "A poet needs a corner," Cyril said, and set a little brass lamp on the bar beside Isaac's notebook, and that was that.

Cyril read the poems sometimes. Only when invited. He was a good reader. He would sit with a page for a long time and then point at a single line and say, "This. The rest is scaffolding." He was right more often than Isaac's workshop had been.

In the winter Cyril installed cameras. A string of black glass domes along the ceiling. A break-in down the block, he said. Everybody was doing it. Isaac noticed one directly over his corner, but it was just a dome, and he forgot about it.

In February Cyril showed him a clip on his phone. It was Isaac, in his corner, at one in the morning, face in his hands, shoulders shaking. Isaac didn't remember crying. He remembered a bad night, a rejection letter, the fourth bourbon.

"I didn't know whether to come over," Cyril said. "I watched you for a while. Upstairs. You looked so alone." He put the phone away. "I just want you to know someone sees you."

Isaac was embarrassed, and touched, and something else, a small cold thing underneath that he decided not to look at.

After that Cyril mentioned things. Little things, kindly. That Isaac chewed his pen when he was close to something good. That he mouthed his lines. That he'd looked at his ex-girlfriend's photos for twenty minutes on Tuesday. "You should let her go," Cyril said. "It's eating you." Isaac had not told anyone about the photos. He had not known the dome could see his screen.

He thought about writing somewhere else. He went to a coffee shop twice. It was bright and loud and nobody set a lamp out for him, and he wrote nothing. When he came back to the lounge Cyril didn't mention his absence. The lamp was lit. His drink was poured. He wrote three pages that night, good ones, and felt relief like a fever breaking.

In April Cyril asked to put a few of the poems on the wall. Framed. With Isaac's name. Isaac said yes, of course. It was the first time anything of his had hung anywhere.

They were the ones from the worst nights. The ones about his father, about the hospital. Cyril had chosen them himself. Isaac hadn't known Cyril had copies. He hadn't known what was in the notebook was something you could copy from a ceiling.

People read them while they waited for drinks. Strangers. Sometimes they looked over at him in his corner, curious, sympathetic, the way you look at an exhibit.

Isaac still writes there. He tilts the notebook away from the dome now. Cyril noticed. He brought over a fresh drink and said gently, "You don't have to hide from me," and Isaac tilted it back.

The Coat

The coat was the first thing Lorne gave Simone, three weeks after he hired her to tend bar at the Sunset Dreams Lounge. Charcoal wool, cut long, with a lining the color of a bruise. A customer had left it years ago, he said, and never come back for it. It fit her as if measured.

"You walk to the bus in that thin thing?" he said. "Not anymore."

She wore it home that night and felt like someone in a film.

The coat had deep pockets. In the first week she found a ticket stub for a concert in 1998 and a book of matches from a hotel in Montreal. She asked Lorne about the woman who'd left it. He said he couldn't remember. He said, "She didn't need it anymore," and went to check on the ice.

Lorne was a fair boss. Better than fair. He paid in cash at the end of every night, generously, and when she was short on rent in her second month he covered it without a word, just tucked an envelope into the coat pocket while it hung on the hook. When she found it at the bus stop she cried a little.

He liked it when she wore the coat. He said so. On the nights she wore her old jacket he was quieter, a little sad, and the tips seemed thinner, and once he scheduled her for the dead Monday shift instead of Friday, and when she asked why he said he hadn't noticed. She started wearing the coat every day.

In winter he began walking her to the bus stop. Then driving her. He'd turn up the heat and say, "You look like you belong in that coat." It sounded like a compliment. She was never quite sure.

In the third month a woman came into the lounge. Late forties, gray roots, a careful face. She ordered a club soda and stared at Simone the whole time she poured it. At the end she said, very quietly, "Where did you get that?"

Simone looked down. She was wearing the coat. She always was now.

Lorne was beside her before she could answer. He was warm, delighted. He said, "Mara. It's been a long time." He said the coat was a gift. He said, "It looks better on her, doesn't it?" He said it kindly. The woman's face did something Simone would spend months trying to describe. Then she put a five on the bar and left without her change.

"Who was that?" Simone asked.

"Someone who used to work here," Lorne said. "She had a hard time letting go of things."

That night, in the coat pocket, Simone found something new. A matchbook from the lounge, and inside the cover, in faded ink, in handwriting not her own: DON'T STAY PAST SPRING.

She held it a long time at the bus stop. Then Lorne's car pulled up, warm, the door already open, and she got in, and when he asked what she was holding she said nothing, nothing at all, and slid the matchbook back into the pocket where she'd found it, where it would keep.

The List

I work the door at the Sunset Dreams Lounge on weekends. There's no cover and no line. Benedict keeps a list anyway, on a clipboard under the podium. Most nights it's just names. Some nights there are little marks beside them. A dot means let them in. A line means don't.

Priya came in every Saturday with her friends. Four women from a law office, loud, happy, the kind of group that makes a room feel alive. Benedict liked them. He sent over flights of whatever they were drinking. He learned all four names in a night. He liked Priya best. Everyone could see it.

In April the marks started.

First it was the friend named Dana. A line beside her name. I asked Benedict, and he said she'd been rude to a server. I hadn't seen it. I turned Dana away, politely, the next Saturday, and the group stood on the sidewalk confused, and then three of them came in without her, because it was raining, because what else were they going to do.

Benedict sat with them that night. He apologized to Priya personally. He said he hated to do it. He said, "Some people, you know, they bring a darkness into a room."

Two weeks later, a line by Kendra. Benedict said she'd been seen with a man who'd caused trouble. Then a line by the last one, Josie, no reason given. "Just trust me," he said. I trusted him. That's the job.

Priya came alone after that. She asked me about it once at the door, her voice tight, and I said it was above my pay grade, and she nodded like she already knew it would be.

Benedict met her inside. He always met her inside now. He said her friends had been jealous of her. He said it was obvious, the way they talked over her. He said she'd outgrown them, and she looked at him like he'd lifted something heavy off her back.

She stopped answering their texts. I know because one of them, Dana, came by the door on a Tuesday, sober, and asked me to give Priya a note. I said I would. I gave it to Benedict. I don't know what he did with it.

In August Priya's name had a little star next to it. I'd never seen a star before. I asked Benedict what it meant.

"It means she's family," he said.

I let her in every night after that. She doesn't stay at the bar anymore. She goes straight back to the office with him, and the door closes. Sometimes, near close, she comes out and stands with me at the podium and looks at the street, at the people walking by in groups, laughing. She never says anything. She just watches them until they're gone.

Last Saturday four young women came up the sidewalk. Nurses, by the look of them. Loud, happy. The one in the middle laughed at something, and the others leaned in toward her.

Benedict came out and stood beside me and watched them for a long moment.

Then he took the clipboard, wrote all four names down carefully, and handed it back to me without a single mark.

Not yet.

The Glass

It's been nine years since I last walked into the Sunset Dreams Lounge, and I still can't drink out of a coupe glass. My husband thinks it's a quirk. I let him.

Maurice had one waiting for me. That's how it began. My third visit, a winter Friday. I was thirty-four and newly transferred to the city and didn't know a soul, and when I sat down at the bar there was a coupe already set out on a little linen square, etched with a crescent moon. "Yours," Maurice said. "If you want it."

I asked why me. He said I had the face of someone who'd been the new girl a lot. I had. Army family. Eleven schools. I'd never told him that.

The glass lived on a shelf behind the bar, apart from the others. I'd watch the bartender reach for it when I came in, before I'd even sat down, and something in me that had been clenched since grade school loosened a little. A place where my glass was waiting.

Maurice filled it himself on the nights he was in. He'd ask about work, my boss, the apartment with the radiator that knocked. He always remembered. He fixed the radiator, actually, sent a man over, wouldn't hear of payment. He said the city could be cold to people who didn't have anyone.

I had people, at first. A woman from work. A man I'd met at a gallery. Maurice was lovely to them when I brought them in. Afterward he'd say small things. That the woman from work seemed competitive with me. That the man from the gallery had looked at the bartender a lot. He'd say it and then wave it off: "I'm overprotective. Ignore me." I couldn't ignore him. That was the whole design.

By the second winter it was mostly just Maurice and me and the glass.

Then one night I came in and the glass wasn't on the shelf. The bartender gave me an ordinary coupe. I looked down the bar and saw mine, the crescent moon catching the light, in front of a young woman I'd never seen, new in town by the look of her, holding it with both hands like something precious.

Maurice found me later. He was sorry. He'd had to lend it out, just for the night. He said, "You understand. You of all people." He said, "Don't make this something it's not."

I stood in that room and felt eleven schools' worth of lunchrooms go quiet around me. I would have done anything to get that glass back. I knew that, standing there. I could feel exactly how much.

That was the night I left, and I didn't go back, and it took me two years to stop driving past.

I'm telling you because last week my niece moved to the city. Thirty-one, knows no one. She texted me a picture from a lounge downtown, amber light, a neon sign. A coupe glass on a linen square, etched with a moon.

"Look," she wrote. "They gave me my own glass."

Index Cards

Leon keeps a box of index cards in the office of the Sunset Dreams Lounge. One card per regular. He writes in pencil so things can change.

FIRST VISIT. Came in alone, 9:40. Sat at the bar but angled toward the door. Ordered something they didn't really want so they'd have something to hold. Checked phone eleven times. No one wrote back. Note: likes to be asked a question and then left alone to answer it.

SECOND VISIT. Same stool. Had the bartender make the same drink without asking. Watched the face. A little flush, pleased, trying not to be. Note: wants to be known but is embarrassed by wanting it. Good.

THIRD VISIT. Sat with them six minutes. Asked about work. They said it was fine. Waited. They said it wasn't fine, actually. Waited again. They told me about the manager, the one who takes credit. Said: "People like that can smell talent. It scares them." Note: they repeated it back to me later, in their own words, as if it were their idea.

FIFTH VISIT. Brought a friend. Friend talked too much, asked where I was from, didn't like the drinks. Comped the table anyway. When the friend went to the restroom, said quietly: "You seem different when they're here." Note: friend has not been back. Subject has not mentioned them.

EIGHTH VISIT. Told me about their mother. The phone calls. The way she says "I'm just worried about you." Said: "Some people love you in a way that keeps you small." Watched them nearly cry. Didn't hand them a napkin. Note: they reached for it themselves and looked at me after, to see whether I'd noticed they were strong. I had. I told them so.

TENTH VISIT. Gave them a reserved card for their stool. Gold script. Watched them touch it.

TWELFTH VISIT. Late. Upset about something at home. Didn't ask what. Said: "You don't have to explain anything here." Locked the door after last call and let them stay until three. Talked about nothing. Note: they called it the best night they'd had in a year.

FOURTEENTH VISIT. Asked them for a small favor. They said yes before I finished. Note: next time ask for something larger, and look embarrassed to be asking.

FIFTEENTH VISIT. Missed a Friday. Sent nothing. When they came in Saturday, stayed at the other end of the bar for an hour. Let them watch me laugh with someone new. When I finally came over, they apologized. For what, they couldn't say. Note: the apology is the important part.

There's one more card in the box, behind all the others. It's blank except for the date.

You found this place on your own. That's what you'll tell yourself later. Nobody led you here. You pressed a button because you were curious, and when it asked whether you were sure, you said yes. Twice. More than twice, maybe. You keep coming back.

Leon has started a card for you.

He hasn't decided yet what you like to drink. But he's noticed that you came in alone, that you've stayed, and that you haven't looked away once, and he's writing that down in pencil, carefully, so it can change.
